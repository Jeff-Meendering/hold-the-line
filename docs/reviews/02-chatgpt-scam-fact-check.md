## Prompt

I'm making a small educational browser game where the player is a bank teller who has to spot customers who are being scammed. Below are the four scam scenarios in the game. For each one, tell me whether it matches how this scam really works, and what is wrong or unrealistic about it.

1. Grandparent scam. A woman in her late 70s withdraws $8,500 in cash. A caller posing as her grandson said he was under arrest, asked her to keep it secret, and is sending a courier to her house to collect the cash in an envelope. She was told to tell the bank it's for a new furnace.

2. Jury duty scam. A man in his mid-20s buys four $500 Visa gift cards at the bank. A caller claiming to be from the sheriff's office said he missed jury duty and will be arrested unless he pays a fine in gift cards. The caller stays on the phone and wants pictures of the backs of the cards. He was told to say they're a wedding present.

3. Romance scam. A woman in her early 60s wires $22,500 to a man in the UK she met on a dating site and has never met in person. He says he's an engineer on an oil rig and asked for the money as a share in his business. He told her to describe him to the bank as a friend and business partner.

4. Bank impersonation scam. A man in his mid-50s closes a CD early and withdraws $14,000 in cash. A caller posing as his bank's fraud department said his accounts were compromised and told him to deposit the cash into a Bitcoin ATM at a gas station by scanning a QR code, to keep it safe. He was told not to trust the branch staff and to say the cash is for a kitchen remodel.

Also three quick questions about the bank side:
a. Is it realistic for a bank or credit union to sell 12 Visa gift cards at $250 each to a small business owner in one visit?
b. Can a customer close a CD early and take $14,000 in cash the same day at a teller window?
c. Do scammers really tell victims what to say to the bank teller, and are tellers trained to ask about it?

Please search the web and give me a link for every claim, preferring the FTC, FBI/IC3, FinCEN, CFPB, or AARP. Quote the sentence from the source that supports each claim. If you can't find a source for something, say so instead of guessing. Only answer what I asked, I don't need new scenario ideas.

Overall: All four scenarios are realistic and closely match documented scam patterns. The strongest details are the demands for secrecy, fabricated emergencies, unusual payment methods, and instructions to mislead bank employees.

### 1. Grandparent scam

Verdict: Very realistic. The scenario closely matches the documented grandparent or family-emergency scam, including the fake arrest, demand for secrecy, urgent cash request, and courier collection.

Supporting sources:

- CFPB — What are some common types of fraud and scams?
  - "If you get a call from someone who says they are your grandchild or another relative, and urgently need money to get out of trouble, it could be a scam."
  - The CFPB also identifies secrecy as a warning sign: "Be careful if a caller insists on secrecy or says they need the money right away."
- Iowa Attorney General — Grandparent and Family-Emergency Scams
  - "The scammer may claim to be a grandchild or other relative who was arrested, injured, hospitalized, stranded, or involved in a crash."
  - "Some schemes send a courier to collect cash from your home or direct you to a drop-off location."

What is realistic: the caller impersonating a grandson; the fabricated arrest; the instruction not to tell anyone; the demand for cash; sending a courier to collect the money.

What is less firmly established: The specific $8,500 amount and the instruction to claim the money is for a new furnace are plausible details, but I did not find an authoritative source documenting those exact details as standard features of this scam. The furnace explanation is consistent with the broader tactic of giving victims a cover story to prevent bank employees or family members from discovering the fraud. I would distinguish that general tactic from the particular furnace example.

### 2. Jury duty scam

Verdict: Very realistic. This is a well-documented scam, and the sheriff's-office impersonation, arrest threat, and gift-card demand are all characteristic.

Supporting sources:

- FTC — Did you get a call or email saying you missed jury duty and need to pay?
  - "This scam usually starts with a phone call or an email from someone claiming to be from the police department or a court official."
  - "They'll tell you there's a warrant out for your arrest, and the only way to cancel it is to pay a fine."
  - "Only scammers insist that you can only pay with gift cards, a payment app, cryptocurrency, or a wire transfer service like Western Union or MoneyGram because it's very hard to get your money back if you pay in these ways."
- FBI Charlotte — Jury Duty Scammers Continue to Target North Carolinians
  - "The scammer also may request that the victim purchase a prepaid card, such as a Green Dot card or gift card, or payment."

What is realistic: a caller impersonating law enforcement; threatening arrest over missed jury duty; demanding immediate payment; requiring gift cards; keeping the victim on the phone to prevent them from checking the story.

What needs qualification: The request for photographs of the backs of the Visa cards is consistent with the underlying gift-card fraud mechanism: scammers need the card numbers and security codes/PINs to redeem the value. The FTC documents scammers asking victims to provide gift-card PIN codes in its romance scam guidance, although I did not locate an authoritative source specifically documenting photographs of the backs of cards in this exact jury-duty scam. The four $500 cards and the wedding-present cover story are plausible, but I did not find authoritative documentation establishing those exact details as standard.

### 3. Romance scam

What is realistic: meeting on a dating site; never having met the person face-to-face; the overseas oil-rig engineer story; asking for money after establishing a romantic connection; requesting a wire transfer; presenting the money as an investment rather than simply asking for a gift.

What is less firmly established: The specific request for a $22,500 share in an oil-rig engineer's business is plausible, particularly given the documented relationship-investment scam pattern. ### 4. Bank impersonation scam

Verdict: Extremely realistic. This is a documented bank-impersonation and cryptocurrency ATM scam. The instruction to withdraw cash and deposit it into a Bitcoin ATM using a supplied QR code is particularly accurate.

Supporting sources:

- FTC — Did someone send you to a Bitcoin ATM? It's a scam

  - "And only a scammer will give you a QR code to 'help' you deposit your life savings in a Bitcoin ATM."

What is realistic: the caller impersonating the bank's fraud department; claiming the accounts have been compromised; telling the customer to withdraw cash to protect it; directing the customer to a Bitcoin ATM; supplying a QR code for the cryptocurrency transaction; creating urgency and demanding secrecy; telling the customer not to trust bank employees.

What is less firmly established: The exact $14,000 amount, early CD closure, gas-station location, and kitchen-remodel cover story are plausible scenario details, but I did not find authoritative sources establishing those precise details as typical elements of this particular scam. The instruction not to trust bank staff is consistent with the broader isolation tactic. The FTC specifically warns that scammers want victims to avoid listening to bank employees.

### Bank-side questions

**a. Could a bank or credit union sell 12 Visa gift cards at $250 each to a small business owner in one visit?**

Yes, this is plausible, but it depends on the financial institution's policies. There is evidence that credit unions sell Visa gift cards with individual values higher than $250.

- Mountain Credit Union — Visa Gift Cards
  - "Available in $5.00 increments with a $25.00 minimum, our VISA Gift Cards can be customized with a value of up to $500.00 each."

What I could not establish: I did not find an authoritative source confirming that banks generally permit a customer to purchase 12 cards in one visit, or that there is a standard industry-wide daily purchase limit. Some institutions may impose transaction limits, card inventory restrictions, or fraud-prevention checks.

My assessment: Your $3,000 purchase is plausible for a small business owner, but don't present it as something every bank would necessarily allow without additional checks.

**b. Can a customer close a CD early and take $14,000 in cash the same day at a teller window?**

Yes, potentially. There is no universal rule guaranteeing same-day cash availability, however. Two separate things matter: whether the bank permits early CD withdrawal, and whether the branch can provide $14,000 in physical cash that day.

- OCC — Can the bank charge me a penalty for withdrawing a CD early?
  - "Banks are generally required by law to assess an early withdrawal penalty whenever funds from a CD are withdrawn prior to the date of maturity."
  - "Read your account agreement for information about your bank's policies and fees."
- Federal Regulation CC — 12 CFR Part 229 (commentary)
  - "For example, some banks require advance notice for large cash withdrawals in order to limit the amount of cash needed to be maintained on hand at any time."

My assessment: Your scenario is realistic if the CD agreement allows early closure, the customer accepts the applicable penalty, the funds are available, and the branch has sufficient cash. $14,000 is not inherently an unrealistic teller transaction. But a branch might require advance notice or offer another way to receive the money. I did not find an authoritative source establishing that $14,000 can always be withdrawn immediately after closing a CD.

**c. Do scammers really tell victims what to say to the bank teller, and are tellers trained to ask about it?**

Yes — directly documented.

- FTC — Will your bank or investment fund stop a transfer to a scammer? Probably not
  - "The scammer, who might even try to keep you on the phone, will tell you to lie, so the bank won't stop your transfer."
  - "The bank teller or employee might not know you, but they do know the signs of fraud."
  - "If they ask, share why you're withdrawing cash."
- CFPB — Recommendations and report for financial institutions on preventing and responding to elder financial exploitation
  - "Train personnel regularly and frequently, and tailor training to specific staff roles."
  - "With a large cash withdrawal ('This is an unusually large withdrawal, are you sure you want cash?')"
  - "With wire transfers, ('Have you taken steps to be sure the recipient is trustworthy?')"
- FinCEN — Advisory on Elder Financial Exploitation
  - "Therefore, it is critical for customer-facing staff to identify and consider the behavioral red flags when conducting transactions involving their older customers."

One important qualification: The CFPB recommendations are guidance and best practices, not a requirement that every teller at every institution follow an identical script.
